In October 1950, Alan Mathison Turing published a 27 page paper in the journal Mind. It opens by asking 'Can machines think?' and then discards the question as unworkable, because defining 'machine' or 'think' by ordinary usage would end in a statistical survey, which he thought absurd. Instead he proposed a game, and he called it the imitation game. The name Turing Test was given to it later by others.

The game has three players: a man, a woman, and an interrogator in another room who asks only written questions, ideally over a teleprinter. The interrogator has to work out which is which from the answers. The man tries to mislead, the woman tries to help. Turing's twist is to ask what happens when a machine takes the man's place. If the interrogator gets confused as often as when two humans play, the machine is indistinguishable, and that is the standard. He attached a specific prediction: within about 50 years, a computer with roughly a billion bits of storage would play the game so well that the average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning. He also took the objections seriously. The most famous, the Lady Lovelace objection, named after Ada Lovelace's note on Babbage's engine, held that a machine can only do what it is told to do and can never originate. Turing's reply was that the assumption that when a fact is presented to a mind all its consequences spring into the mind at once is a fallacy which philosophers and mathematicians are particularly subject to, a very useful assumption under many circumstances, 'but one too easily forgets that it is false'.

The game has outlived its author by three quarters of a century and is still the default yardstick for whether a conversational machine counts as intelligent. Critics never stopped arguing against it: fooling the interrogator shows only that the machine imitates conversation, not that it understands or thinks. No system has cleanly met Turing's exact 70 percent, five minute standard under his conditions; most demonstrations are small trials. But the underlying move, replacing an unanswerable metaphysical question with an observable test, has shaped the whole field, and it is why modern language models still make the news. Each time someone says a model 'thinks', they are quietly answering the question Turing set down in 1950.
