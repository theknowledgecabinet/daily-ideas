A month to find an apartment? Spend the first 11 days without a checkbook in hand, then put a deposit on the first place that beats everything you have seen. Eleven days out of thirty is roughly 37 percent of the month, and that fraction turns out to be the right one for a whole class of problems called optimal stopping. The same rule covers a job search, a partner, a parking spot, or anything else where options arrive one at a time and you cannot go back for a rejected one.

The logic is easy to state. Stop too early and you might miss something better. Keep looking too long and the best option you already saw is gone. Skip the first 37 percent of what comes your way purely to get a feel for what good looks like, then commit to the next option that beats the best of that first batch. That gives you the best mathematical chance of ending up with the overall best one. The 37 percent comes from a theorem about sequential decisions, not from folk wisdom. Tom Griffiths, a cognitive scientist at UC Berkeley, and the computer scientist Brian Christian apply it to examples ranging from Darwin choosing a wife to Katy Perry managing 82 million Twitter followers. The Berkeley article notes they interviewed nearly 100 experts for the research.

The authors raise their own caveat: the rule assumes you can recognise the best option you have seen so far, and that you roughly know how long your window is. The Berkeley article mentions cautionary tales of scientists trying to optimise their love lives, with mixed results.
