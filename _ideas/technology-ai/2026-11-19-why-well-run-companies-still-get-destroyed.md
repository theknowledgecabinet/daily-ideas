Clayton Christensen studied how whole industries die, and his starting point was the hard disk drive industry in the 1990s. What he found ran counter to the usual story. Firms do not usually fall behind because they fail to keep up with the technology. The disk drive makers knew about the smaller, cheaper drives arriving. The problem was that those drives did not make enough money to justify producing them, and building them would have pulled resources away from the work that was actually paying the bills. The rational response was to ignore them, and that is precisely what got the leaders killed.

This is the innovator's dilemma. A disruptive innovation, in Christensen's terms, starts in a place the incumbent does not value. It enters at the low end of a market, where customers are content with a good enough product and will not pay a premium, or it serves a market that did not exist before. These products tend to be technologically straightforward, off the shelf components in a simpler package. They offer less of what mainstream customers want, which is exactly why the established firms do not bother. Then the newcomer improves, moves upmarket, and the incumbent, locked into the economics of its own customers, can at best make a me too entry where survival is the only reward.

His classic example is not the first car but the Model T. Late nineteenth century automobiles were expensive luxury items that did not touch the horse drawn carriage market. Then Ford's mass production changed the economics, and the carriage industry was displaced.

There is a further twist. Christensen argued that the standard advice to listen to the customer can be strategically counterproductive, because the customers you serve are the ones whose needs blind you to the market forming at the bottom. And the idea itself has been contested. Scholars such as Jill Lepore have pointed out that some supposed victims, including Seagate and US Steel, remain dominant in their industries, and the word disruptive is now used so loosely it barely means anything. Christensen later tightened the definition: disruption is a process running from the margins to the mainstream, not a product or a label for whatever upsets an industry.
