Harari's argument in Sapiens is that our species took over the world around 70,000 years ago because we are the only animals that can talk about things that do not exist. Other animals use their communication to describe things that actually exist, the river, the predator, the ripe fruit. We use ours to create new realities: myths, money, laws, nations, gods. Once enough people believe the same story, strangers can cooperate as if it were true, and that is what makes large scale cooperation possible.

His favourite example is money. A dollar bill has no value except in our collective imagination, yet everyone agrees to treat it as real, so it becomes one of the most reliable systems of trust ever invented. Religion works the same way, he says, and by that he means not only the faiths in gods but any set of norms founded on laws believed to be superhuman, from the Christian God to the "natural laws" of economics. The function is identical: give stability to human institutions.

The flip side of the trick is that the stories are never neutral. Most of the imagined orders that have divided us into classes, races and castes claim to rest on natural or divine facts, and those claims make the divisions feel permanent. Harari points out that they are not permanent at all. The French Revolution replaced one fiction, that aristocrats are born superior, with another, that all people are equal. Since these orders are stories and not biology, they can be changed by people who tell a new one.

His closing thought in the Smithsonian interview is blunt. "A nation never suffers. A corporation never suffers. Only individuals suffer." The grand stories we tell are the only way large societies can work, but they do not feel real to the people inside them.
