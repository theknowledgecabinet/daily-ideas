There is a popular story that writing was invented by bureaucrats. The state needed to track people, land and harvests, so elites devised a script, and poetry and love letters came later. The political scientist James Scott took this line to its end in his 2017 book Against the Grain, arguing that the state is a recording machine and that without the state there would be no writing.

The archaeological record makes this harder to accept. In Mesopotamia, the earliest cuneiform tablets (circa 3300 to 2900 BCE) were mostly used to run temple workshops, with little in the way of state functions, as David Wengrow has pointed out. Counting came first. The archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat showed that clay tokens, one cone per unit of grain, one diamond per unit of honey, were in use for centuries before any state emerged. People kept them in hollow clay balls, then pressed them into the wet surface so the contents could be checked without opening the ball, and then dropped the ball altogether and pressed tokens straight into flat clay tablets. Tokens predate the state by roughly 2,000 years, and early states managed without writing for nearly 3,000 years because the token system did the counting.

Elsewhere the evidence is thinner. In China, oracle bones from about 1320 BCE are the oldest texts we have. In Mesoamerica, the earliest writing is engraved on rock and painted on murals, and the scenes are mostly kings and ritual acts, with no economic records at all. Writing was also reinvented outside states entirely: Shong Lue Yang, a Hmong farmer in the mountains of Vietnam in the late 1950s, devised a syllabary for his language and revised it four times, each version more linguistically sophisticated than the last.

So writing behaves less like a weapon the state built and more like gunpowder, which alchemists stumbled on. The state certainly expanded it: scripts that were bound to rituals and chants became flexible, everyday writing when governments needed names, contracts and letters to distant garrisons. But its roots lie in counting, temple bookkeeping and the human habit of pressing marks into wet clay.
