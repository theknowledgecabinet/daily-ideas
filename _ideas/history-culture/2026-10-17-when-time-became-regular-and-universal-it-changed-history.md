- Your calendar descends from one decision in 311 BCE. The Seleucid Era, begun by Seleucus I in Babylon, is the direct ancestor of every era system, including the Common Era.
- A shared timeline changed what people could imagine. Before it, dates were local and tied to rulers' reigns; after it, anyone could name and hold in their head a date decades or centuries in the future.
- Endless time provoked a reaction. The first apocalyptic texts emerged inside the Seleucid empire, and the essay argues this was because subject peoples rebelled against a chronology that never ended.

Before 311 BCE, nobody could write down a simple year. In ancient Mesopotamia, events were dated by what happened: the year king Naram-Sin reached the sources of the Tigris and Euphrates, the name of the consul or magistrate in office, or the 40th year of Nebuchadnezzar. When Thucydides dated the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War, he had to chain together the year of a priestess at Argos, the magistrate at Athens, the magistrate at Sparta, the season and the time of night. There was no shared timeline that crossed borders.

After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, one of his generals, Seleucus I Nicator, did what nobody had done. He started a count from his own arrival in Babylon in the spring of 311 BCE, and the count simply went on. When Seleucus died, his son did not restart the count. It kept increasing, year on year. The Seleucid Era was the first chronology with no end: a number that never restarted, reversed or stopped. Every era system since, including our own, descends from it.

Two things made the change work. The year was recorded as pure number, stable across Greek, Akkadian, Phoenician and Aramaic scripts, so it held together across an empire from Bulgaria to Afghanistan. And the numbers were stamped on everything: market weights, jar handles, coins, temple offerings, tombstones, tax receipts, marriage contracts. For the first time, an entire society lived inside dated objects.

The odd part is what came next. Kosmin's argument is that the earliest apocalyptic texts in history appeared inside this empire, and nowhere else. When Daniel dreams of the statue made of gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay, with a stone not cut by human hands destroying it, history appears as a closed totality: ordered, head to toe, with an ending. The essay claims this is a reaction to the endless calendar. The only way to stop a timeline that never ends is to insist that time itself ends.
