Years ago, a Papua New Guinean politician named Yali asked the young anthropologist Jared Diamond a question that never left him: why is it that white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, while black people had little cargo of their own? Diamond built his 1997 book around that question, and his short answer is geography.

The engine is farming. The first farms appeared about 11,500 years ago in the Fertile Crescent and, shortly after, in China, because those places happened to hold the greatest variety of wild plants and animals that were both useful and possible to domesticate. A farm feeds far more people than the same acre of forest, and settled villages can hold more people than roaming hunters. Once there is a surplus, a society can feed people who do not farm: specialists, bureaucrats, scribes, kings. That is where technology, writing and empires come from. Hunter-gatherers cannot afford those posts.

The axis of a continent decides how far the surplus spreads. Eurasia runs east to west, so crops and techniques moved along similar latitude, similar climate and similar seasons, from the Fertile Crescent into Europe and into China. Africa and the Americas run north to south, and the journey keeps crossing deserts, mountain ranges and ice fields, where useful plants cannot survive. On top of that, thirteen of the fourteen herd domestic animals were Eurasian species, and most of the great epidemic diseases came from them. European children caught those diseases young and lived through them; when smallpox reached the Americas it found almost no immune people. An estimate puts 95 per cent of Native American deaths down to disease rather than to the sword.

The criticism is fair, and Diamond concedes much of it. Historians and anthropologists have called the book too deterministic, leaving almost no room for human choice, and economists such as Acemoglu and Robinson argue that institutions matter more. Diamond's reply is that understanding the causes is itself a tool: if you know what is making a place poor, you can do something about it. That is a better reading of the book than one that makes people seem doomed by their latitude.
