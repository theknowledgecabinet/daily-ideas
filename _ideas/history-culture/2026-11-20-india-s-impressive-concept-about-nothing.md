Carved into the rock face of a small ninth-century temple inside Gwalior Fort in central India is the number 270. For a long time it was the oldest known example of zero written as a digit. It sits in the Chaturbhuj Temple, a modest structure tucked between much larger towers, and it marks the moment when a culture decided that nothing could be a number.

BBC Travel correspondent Mariellen Ward traces the invention to a cultural habit. Hindu and Buddhist traditions were already comfortable with the idea of nothingness, and the Buddhist doctrine of Shunyata is essentially the practice of emptying the mind. Peter Gobets, of the Netherlands-based ZerOrigIndia Foundation, has suggested that the mathematical zero, shunya in Sanskrit, may have grown out of that same tradition of emptiness. Indian mathematicians also played with scale the Greeks did not. They counted into the trillions, where the Greeks stopped at about 10,000, and they had more than one kind of infinity.

The record keeps moving back. Ward reports that the Bakhshali manuscript, an Indian scroll that uses a dot as a placeholder for an empty column, has recently been carbon dated to the third or fourth century, making it the earliest recorded occurrence of zero. Marcus du Sautoy, a professor of mathematics at Oxford, calls the creation of zero as a number in its own right one of the greatest breakthroughs in the history of mathematics. The consequences reach your pocket: zero and one are the digits of binary, the system every computer is built on. And by the second or third century BCE, the musicologist Pingala had already described binary for prosody, the metre of verse, as the historian of science Subhash Kak notes.

Elsewhere, the idea did not take. The Babylonians and the Maya had zero as a placeholder, but the idea of zero as a usable number does not seem to have developed outside India. Ward reports that at one point in early Christian Europe, religious leaders banned the use of zero altogether. If God is in everything, a symbol for nothing must be satanic.
