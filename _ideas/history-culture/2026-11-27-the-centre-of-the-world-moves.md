Peter Frankopan has a memory that has never left him. He was a schoolboy in Britain, learning about Henry VIII for the third or fourth time, the Battle of Hastings, the First World War. At the same time, the news was full of Soviet missiles in eastern Germany, the revolution in Iran, the Khmer Rouge. He could not understand why the parts of the world making the headlines were the parts of the world he was never taught about.

The Silk Roads is his answer: a history of the world told from the centre rather than the edge. Frankopan opens with maps, because every culture draws itself in the middle of the world. A child in Shanghai does not put London where we do. Once the centre of the map moves, the old story quietly rearranges itself. The great engine of scholarship and culture for a thousand years sat in the Middle East, while Western Europe, in Frankopan's own blunt words, was a bystander, completely irrelevant in trade, scholarship and ideas.

One of his favourite examples is the Byzantine Empire. We still use the word Byzantine to mean smoke and mirrors and shady dealing, but the empire in question lasted more than a thousand years and was multilingual and multi-ethnic, and it managed an administrative system Frankopan thinks the European Union could learn from. It is, in his words, the single most successful political entity in history. He is fond of De Administrando Imperio, a 10th century manual by the emperor Constantine VII, which records the names of the rocks where Viking traders on the Russian rivers could smash their boats. Frankopan calls it CIA-style intelligence gathering, thousands of miles from Constantinople.
