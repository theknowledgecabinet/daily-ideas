takeaways:
- The productive sequence is attempt first, instruction second. The failed attempt is not time wasted before the real lesson; it is the preparation that makes the lesson stick.
- The number of solutions a learner generates before instruction predicts how much they will learn from it. The attempts do not need to be good, only varied.
- The benefit comes from deliberate design, not from suffering. Struggle followed by instruction that builds on the struggle is the lesson. Struggle followed by a lecture that ignores it is just wasted time.
caveats: The strongest evidence is from mathematics education, with the mixed results for younger pupils noted above. The effect depends on the instruction afterwards actually engaging with the learners' attempts, so it is a design choice rather than a universal law.
try_this: Next time you meet a genuinely new problem at work or in study, spend ten minutes generating two or three plausible approaches on paper before opening the manual, the docs or the answer key. Jot down what you assumed along the way, then read the right approach and compare it against your own notes.

Most of us have been taught the same way. The teacher explains the concept, we copy the method, then we practise. It feels orderly. Manu Kapur, a learning scientist at ETH Zurich, has been quietly testing the reverse for nearly two decades, and the results keep pointing the other way.

His design is called productive failure. Students get a genuinely hard problem before they have been taught the concept they need to solve it. They will fail to find the correct answer, but they are expected to generate as many approaches as they can and try to improve them together. Only then does the instruction arrive, and it uses the students' own attempts as the starting point.

Kapur's reasoning has two parts. First, when a concept is new, learners often lack the background to understand why the parts of the method fit together. They can copy the steps without seeing the structure. Second, when they have built their own attempts, even poor ones, the later explanation lands somewhere. They can compare what they did with the canonical solution and understand why the official method is assembled the way it is. In his studies, typically on concepts like standard deviation, students who had attempted the problem first outperformed directly taught students on understanding and transfer to new problems, without losing procedural fluency.

The caveat is important, and he is blunt about it in a 2024 Education Week interview. A meta-analysis of 53 studies found that problem-solving before instruction generally helps, but the effect disappears if the struggle is not deliberately designed. Unstructured flailing followed by a lecture is not the same thing. In the analysis, younger pupils (grades 2 to 5) did not show the benefit, largely because the instruction that followed did not fold their attempts into the lesson. The value lives in the sequencing and the scaffolding, not in the failure itself.
