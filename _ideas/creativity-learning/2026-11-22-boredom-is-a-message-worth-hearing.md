For most of history, boredom has been treated as something to escape, a gap to fill. A growing body of research suggests something else: boredom is a signal worth taking seriously. James Danckert, a cognitive neuroscientist at the University of Waterloo, compares it to pain. Pain tells you something is wrong and that you should act about it, and boredom plays a similar role, telling you that what you are doing right now is not working. Erin Westgate, a social psychologist at Florida State University, gives the signal a more precise shape. The task in front of you is not a good fit for the mental or emotional resources you have at that moment. It is too easy, or too hard, or it simply does not matter to you.

The reading extends beyond the tedious spreadsheet to the shape of a life. Westgate points out that we are loath to change things, slow to quit a job, a relationship, a town. Boredom is the pressure gauge that tells you the change may be necessary. Teachers see the same mechanism in classrooms, where children get bored when material is too easy or too hard, and researchers talk about a Goldilocks zone for attention. One practical fix is the utility-value intervention developed by Chris Hulleman at the University of Virginia, in which a lesson is connected to something the student already cares about. A budding chef who is bored to tears by mathematics can start seeing fractions as a way of baking a cake.

This is where the creativity angle gets interesting, and where the article is unusually honest. You may have heard that boredom makes you more creative, and some early studies found as much, but they have not been replicated. A 2016 study found that boredom probably does not generate creativity, that practice does, and that in many cases boredom actually impaired fluency on creative tasks. Andreas Elpidorou, a philosopher of the mind at the University of Louisville, draws the distinction that matters. What produces the ideas is the response, and it is the person who deserves the credit. His summary is blunt: "I would not praise the boredom. I would praise you."

Danckert's suggestion is to read the signal. Pause, take a breath, ask why you are bored, and decide deliberately what to do next instead of defaulting to the phone. Call a friend you have lost touch with, take a walk, pick up an instrument you have been meaning to practice. Elpidorou is careful to note that not everyone has good options available, and that the freedom to wander is itself a kind of privilege. But if you have some say in how you spend your attention, that itchy boredom is an invitation to recalibrate.
