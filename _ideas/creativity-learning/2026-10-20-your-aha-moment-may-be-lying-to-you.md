Most people have a story about an idea arriving fully formed in a shower or on a walk. Jonathan Schooler, a psychologist at UC Santa Barbara, wanted to know whether the rush that comes with such moments is actually a good sign. He surveyed creative writers and physicists daily for two weeks and asked them to note their ideas and when they arrived. About a fifth of each day's most important ideas came while the participants were mind-wandering, not working on the task at hand, and those ideas were more likely to carry an aha moment, often breaking an impasse.

So far, so good: the eureka feel seems to track with ideas that arrive. Then Schooler asked the participants to revisit the same ideas six months later and rate them again. The ideas still rated as creative, but less important than they had felt at the moment of capture. The aha experience is probably a visceral marker that an important idea is on its way, but it also inflates the importance of ideas that are not. In Schooler's words, we have to be careful of false ahas. The bias has a plausible reason: related work by John Kounios found that insight solutions produced a burst of activity in the orbitofrontal cortex, the brain's pleasure region, especially in people who are highly sensitive to rewards. The pleasure of insight is itself rewarding, which makes it a poor judge of the idea that caused it.

The practical move is to treat an aha as a flag, not a verdict. Jot the idea down, get on with your day, and come back to it days or weeks later to judge it on its merits rather than on the strength of the feeling.
