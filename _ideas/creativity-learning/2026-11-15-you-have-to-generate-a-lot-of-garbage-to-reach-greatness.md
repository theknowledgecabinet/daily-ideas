In 2016, Wharton professor Adam Grant published Originals: How Non-Conformists Move the World. In a sit-down interview with Knowledge at Wharton, he walked through what the research actually says about where original ideas come from, and one finding surprised him most: quantity and quality turn out to be closely related.

He points to the psychologist Dean Simonton, whose career-long studies find that the number of ideas a person produces is one of the best predictors of their eventual creative output and of how they are regarded by history. The more attempts you make, the more variety you get. Most of them will be dead ends, but each one widens the odds of stumbling on something that genuinely works.

Grant's illustration comes from the composers. If you compare Beethoven, Bach and Mozart with their contemporaries, the difference is less that their average work was so much better than everyone else's. It is that they produced 600 to 1,000 different compositions, a handful of which became masterpieces. The same pattern shows up inside a single career: creators tend to be at their most original precisely in the stretches when they are also having their most failures.

Think of Thomas Edison. He made a talking doll so creepy it frightened adults, tried a fruit preservation technique that flopped, and failed at mining. It was during a run of more than a hundred failed ideas that he perfected the lightbulb. The implication Grant draws is uncomfortable for anyone who polishes one idea for years: the perfectionist path tends to starve the process of the raw material that breakthroughs need.
