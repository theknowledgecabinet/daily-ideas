Rick Rubin produced the early albums of Metallica, Johnny Cash, Adele and Jay-Z, and started Def Jam out of his college dorm room. In The Creative Act: A Way of Being (2023) he sets out a simple map of how creative work actually unfolds. It runs through four phases, and each one has different rules: seed, experimentation, craft, and completion.

The first two phases are wide open. In the seed phase you gather raw material without judging it, just noticing what feels alive. In the experimentation phase you let that material grow on its own and see where it wants to go. Rubin is blunt about this: these stages cannot be rushed, and they take as long as they take. He spent the first four years of writing the book simply gathering the material, piling up around a thousand pages of notes, and the next three years figuring out how to present it.

The last two phases work differently. Once the seed has taken, the work enters the craft phase, where you can finally see what the thing is, and then the completion phase, where you refine it. This is where the counterintuitive part lands. Rubin admits that finishing has always been the hardest part for him, and that he used to treat a deadline as an enemy. He has since learned that a deadline only harms the work at the beginning, when you are still gathering and experimenting. In the final stretch it helps. "Once you really have the code cracked and it's just down to putting the frame on it," he told Levitt, "you can do that on deadline and it will help the work not hurt the work."

The useful bit is that it gives you permission to drop the guilt. You are not slow or lazy when the idea is still in the seed phase. It is supposed to take its time. The pressure to get it done by the deadline mostly belongs to the last part of the job, not to the whole thing.
