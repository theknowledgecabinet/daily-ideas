In The Power of Habit, the central idea is that every habit, no matter how complicated, runs on the same three-part loop. There is a cue, a trigger that tells your brain to go on automatic. Then there is the routine, the behaviour itself, the part we usually mean when we say that something has become a habit. And at the end there is a reward, something your brain enjoys, which is what makes it file the whole sequence away for next time.

Neuroscientists trace the habit-making to a region called the basal ganglia. As a behaviour repeats, that part takes over and the part of the brain that makes conscious decisions basically switches off. That is why you can parallel park while holding a conversation. The skill has stopped needing you. The catch is that the loop is stubborn. If the cues stay the same, the routine stays the same, and changing your surroundings is often the easiest way to interrupt it, which is why a break away feels so different.

The more useful part is how to change a habit. You cannot delete one, but you can replace it. Duhigg calls it the golden rule of habit change: keep the same cue and the same reward, but swap in a new routine. If you know the moment that sets off the old habit and the payoff you were actually after, you can slot a better behaviour in at exactly that point.

One wrinkle makes this harder in practice. We are often wrong about what we are actually craving. A smoker thinks it is nicotine, when what they really want might be the break, the walk to the window, the excuse to talk to someone. Get the reward wrong and the new routine fails, because it never delivers what the cue is asking for.
