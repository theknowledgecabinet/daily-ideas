takeaways:
- 'Praise what the person did, not who they are: telling a child they worked hard on something lands differently from telling them they are clever.'
- A single compliment can quietly change behaviour, because it signals which label the person now has to defend.
- The habit worth building is one that treats struggling as information, not as proof of a fixed limit.
caveats: These are lab studies with school children, and the praise was a one off in an artificial setting, so the sizes of the effects are hard to generalise. Later meta analyses of growth mindset training in schools have found smaller, more mixed effects than these early studies suggest, so the finding is directionally solid but the real world impact is more modest than it first looks.
try_this: Next time you praise someone, or yourself, swap the label for the process. Instead of saying they are so quick at this, say what you actually saw, such as that they kept working at it until the piece fit. Takes seconds, but it points the credit somewhere that can grow.

Carol Dweck, the Stanford psychologist behind the phrase "growth mindset", has a simple-sounding finding that keeps surprising people. In a series of studies with fifth graders, she and her colleague Claudia Mueller gave children a set of puzzles, then split them into three groups and praised them in three different ways. One third of the children were told "you must be smart at this". Another third were told "you must have tried really hard", and the last third got a flat "good job". The praise changed what the children did next.

When the children were offered a follow-up task, they could pick something easy in their comfort zone, or something hard and new where they might fumble and learn. The children told they were smart largely chose the easy route. They had a label to protect now, and a mistake risked losing it. The children praised for effort went for the hard task, again and again. When the problems then got genuinely difficult, the "smart" group's confidence collapsed. If success meant being clever, struggling meant they were not. Their performance kept slipping. The effort-praised group held steady, kept enjoying the problems, and kept improving.

The most striking detail is what came after. The children were asked to write an anonymous note to a pupil at another school and report their scores. About forty per cent of the children who had been praised for intelligence lied about how they had done, and only in the flattering direction. Even in an anonymous note to a stranger they would never meet, admitting the stumble was too humiliating. Dweck's point is that this is what happens when a person's value is tied to a fixed trait: the mistake becomes something to hide rather than something to address.
