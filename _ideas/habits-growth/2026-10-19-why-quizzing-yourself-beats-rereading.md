- Rereading and highlighting create an illusion of mastery that fades quickly, which is exactly why they feel so satisfying in the moment.
- The effect grows when retrieval is spaced out over time and takes a little effort, instead of being done all in one sitting.
caveats: The testing effect is one of the better-established findings in cognitive psychology, but most of the cited evidence comes from classroom and laboratory studies with students on factual and conceptual material. Cognitive scientists have spent decades noticing something that runs against how most of us study. When people reread a text and highlight the key lines, they feel like they know the material. That feeling is the problem. Rereading makes the page look familiar, and familiarity gets mistaken for learning. Researchers call this the illusion of mastery.

Henry Roediger, a psychologist at Washington University, ran one of the cleanest versions of this idea. He gave different groups of students the same material and split their time between studying and being tested. One group did the same loop over and over: study, then test, study, then test. Another group studied more and tested less. A third just studied, with no tests at all. If you asked them, the students who did the most studying felt the most confident. But a week later, the opposite was true. The group that had been quizzed the most remembered the most, and the group that had only reread the material did the worst (Zaromb & Roediger, 2010).

The reason is that testing does two jobs at once. It checks what you know, and the effort of pulling an answer out of your head also strengthens it. The memory changes when you retrieve it, so the next retrieval is easier. Rereading does almost none of that work. It keeps information available in the short term, which is why it feels like it is working, but it fades quickly. The effect gets stronger when the retrieval is effortful and spaced out over time, rather than done all in one sitting. That is also why the idea feels so counterintuitive: the strategy that works best is the one that feels hardest in the moment.
