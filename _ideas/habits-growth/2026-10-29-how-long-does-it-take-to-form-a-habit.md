Twenty-one days is a number that has outlived every study that produced it. A more careful answer comes from a study run at University College London, where Phillippa Lally and her colleagues followed 96 people who each picked a single healthy behaviour to do once a day, like drinking a glass of water, taking a vitamin, or doing 50 sit-ups. They did it in the same context, such as after breakfast, and logged each day whether they had done it and how automatic it felt. After 12 weeks, 82 of them supplied enough data, and the team fitted a growth curve to each person's sense of automaticity. The median time to reach 95 per cent of the way to full automaticity was 66 days.

The range is the detail worth keeping. One participant plateaued in 18 days, while the slowest was forecast to need 254. The behaviour's complexity played a part, with the exercise group taking roughly a half again as long as the eating and drinking groups to level off. The cue does most of the work. Lally's team stress that a habit is a behaviour performed automatically because it has been repeated in the same situation, so the context itself starts the action. Keep the trigger steady, such as with the morning coffee, and automaticity climbs along a curve that flattens near its ceiling. The final strength of the habit seems set by something more than raw repetition: four people whose curves modelled well but who ended weak had performed the behaviour far less often than the others.

The part most attempts depend on is what the study says about the day you miss, where most people give up. The team looked at individual missed opportunities and found they cut a participant's automaticity score by under half a point. When the person did the behaviour again the next day, their scores climbed from the level of the day before the miss. A single lapse did not materially change the process. Chronic inconsistency is another matter: roughly half the participants were not consistent enough to reach habit status at all. One skipped day is noise in the data. A pattern of skips is what keeps a behaviour from ever becoming yours.
