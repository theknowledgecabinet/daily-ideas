Most of us do not fail because we are lazy. We fail because we planned the wrong thing. When you tell yourself you want to exercise more, you have set a destination. But between that goal and actually lacing up your trainers on a tired Tuesday evening sits a gap, and inside that gap the decision has to be made in the moment, and in the moment we usually do not make it.

The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer, at New York University, spent decades on exactly this. His answer is a single rewording. Instead of "I will exercise more", you write "If it is 7 a.m. on Monday, then I will put on my running shoes and go out for twenty minutes." That second sentence is what he called an implementation intention, also known as if-then planning. It binds a concrete cue (the alarm) to a concrete response (the shoes, the door) before the moment arrives. Gollwitzer argued that this creates something he called strategic automaticity. The cue alone now triggers the behaviour, without you having to decide to act. You no longer carry the weight of the decision; the plan carries it for you.

The effect is real and it has been replicated a great deal. In one of Gollwitzer's early studies, students who named a difficult personal project and formed an if-then plan finished it 62 percent of the time, against 22 percent of those who only set the goal. A 2006 meta-analysis pooled 94 studies with more than 8,000 people and found a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment. The trick still works when your mind is otherwise occupied, which is why it holds up in real life where raw willpower does not.

The honest version matters. In 2025, the same team ran a far bigger meta-analysis across 642 tests, and after correcting for studies that never got published the average effect on behaviour came out modest rather than dramatic. And the plan only works if you genuinely hold the goal it serves. A vague cue like "when I have time" does nothing, because nothing specific is left to fire. And it shines on small, startable actions, such as taking a pill or opening a file, far more than on big, branching ones, such as finishing a thesis. It is a tool for starting and for shielding, not a replacement for motivation.
