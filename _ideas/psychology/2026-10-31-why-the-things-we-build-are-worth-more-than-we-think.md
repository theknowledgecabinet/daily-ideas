You spend a Saturday assembling a bookshelf, and the back panel is slightly crooked. When a friend asks what it is worth, the number in your head is well above the price of a factory-finished piece with identical dimensions. That gap is the IKEA effect, named after the Swedish retailer whose furniture arrives in flat packs precisely so you do the building.

Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely formalised the idea in a 2011 paper in the Journal of Consumer Psychology. They ran four studies. In one, participants folded origami cranes and frogs and then bid on their own creations alongside paper folded by experts. The builders valued their lopsided paper animals at roughly five times what non-builders were willing to pay, while the expert origami fetched only a little more. The same pattern appeared with IKEA boxes and Lego sets.

Two boundary conditions sharpen the finding. The task must be successfully completed: participants who finished their box valued it far more than those stopped at half the steps, and people who built a creation only to be made to dismantle it lost the valuation bump entirely. The effect also does not require personalisation. The boxes and Lego sets allowed no customisation, yet the overvaluation still showed up.

The researchers controlled for the endowment effect (simply owning something raises its value) and for effort justification (we rationalise hard work by inflating the outcome). What remained was the labour itself, done by people who did not consider themselves DIYers. The BBC piece closes with the marketing angle: meal kits, "ready-to-create" food options and DIY furniture all lean on the effect to make customers feel they have got value for money.
