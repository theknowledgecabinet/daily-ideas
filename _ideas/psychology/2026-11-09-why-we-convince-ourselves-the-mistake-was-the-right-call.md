Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson are both social psychologists, and in their book Mistakes Were Made (But Not By Me) they make one idea do the heavy lifting: we do not update our beliefs when the evidence arrives. We repair them instead. When an action turns out badly, or a belief no longer holds up, the mind does not simply file the loss. It rebuilds the story until the mistake looks justifiable, or until it can be pushed onto someone else. The psychologists call the driving tension cognitive dissonance, a term that traces back to Leon Festinger, and the book is essentially a tour of what that tension makes us do.

Take the case Festinger is famous for. He slipped into a doomsday group convinced the world would end on a specific date, and expected that when the date passed without catastrophe the members would abandon their prophet. The opposite happened. The people who had sold their houses and given away their money became the most fervent believers. It is the same logic on a smaller scale: back a war, then learn the reason you were given for it was false, and you first refuse to accept the absence of the evidence, and then argue that there were other good reasons all along. Or buy the pricier appliance and spend the following weeks quietly settling on the view that the cheap one would never have lasted.

It gets darker the closer the habit gets to other people. If one part of you believes you are a decent person and another knows you hurt someone, the mind resolves the clash by deciding that the person deserved it. The authors follow this small step by small step, the way a child who hits a younger brother tells himself the blow was justified, and how that circle of justification feeds more aggression. Their own summary is blunt: if mistakes were made, memory helps us remember that they were made by someone else.
