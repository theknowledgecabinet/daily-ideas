takeaways:
- Recent gains make people more willing to take risks, because the money is coded as winnings in its own account rather than as their own.
- Losses create a pull to break even, which can make a fair or unfavourable bet look reasonable right after a setback.
- Identical money can feel different depending on which mental account it belongs to, which changes what counts as a good deal.
caveats: 'The house money and break-even effects come from real money experiments, mostly in gambling and laboratory settings with modest sums. In Misbehaving, Richard Thaler builds his case around a simple observation: when people think about money, they do not treat it all the same. The idea he calls mental accounting is that we sort money into internal accounts, a jar for rent, one for food, one for gambling, and each account runs its own rules. The official Nobel Prize overview of his work retells a story he loves: in their starving artist days, Gene Hackman visited Dustin Hoffman's apartment and asked for a loan. On the kitchen counter stood several mason jars, each labelled, rent, utilities, and so on. Hackman could not work out how someone with so many full jars could need a loan, until Hoffman pointed at the empty one. It was the food jar.

The consequences get stranger than they first sound. Thaler and Eric Johnson ran real money experiments and found two effects. The house money effect: after a gain, people become more willing to take risks, because the winnings sit in their own mental account and are treated differently from other money. The break-even effect: after a loss, people reach for bets that offer a chance of getting back to even. Thaler's own favourite everyday pair is the jacket and the calculator. You are buying a jacket for $15 and a calculator for $125, and the calculator is $5 cheaper at a branch a 20 minute drive away. Most people make the trip. Now the same deal with the prices reversed, jacket $125, calculator $15, and most do not. The saving is identical. The account it belongs to changed.

The same logic stretches beyond spending. Thaler's colleagues found that New York taxi drivers behave as if each day is a separate account with a target income, so they drive less when demand is high. Investors, held up by the same idea, are more likely to sell a stock that has risen than one that has fallen, because selling is how the account gets closed and the loss feels real.

The practical upshot is small but real. The account you put money in changes how it feels to spend or risk it, and you choose that account before you think about it.
