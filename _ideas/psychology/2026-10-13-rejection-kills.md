The brain barely distinguishes a cut from a slight. In the 2003 CyberBall experiment, Naomi Eisenberger's team put people in an fMRI scanner playing a virtual ball-tossing game with two others who, after a while, started passing only to each other, excluding the participant. The exclusion was programmed by a computer, yet it reliably lit up the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, the same regions that register the raw unpleasantness of physical injury. The more distress people reported, the stronger the pain-related activity.

Evolutionarily, that makes sense: for most of our history, being cut off from the group was not an embarrassment but a survival threat, so the brain borrowed the existing threat-and-pain signal rather than inventing a new one.

What happens after the sting matters more. Roy Baumeister found that some people, right after rejection, don't feel hurt at all. They go numb in an 'ego-shock' and then act more aggressively, take more risks and help less.

The damage then migrates into the body: chronic social rejection is linked to a rise in inflammation and to poorer health broadly, a thread the essay traces through the Whitehall civil-service studies, which tied lower status and isolation to early death. There's a final, unsettling turn: rejection need not be real to keep hurting. The brain can re-run the exclusion over and over, fed by our own rumination, which is how loneliness and self-doubt become self-sustaining even when no one is actually slighting you.
