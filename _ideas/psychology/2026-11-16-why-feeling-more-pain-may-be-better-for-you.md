In 1993 Daniel Kahneman and colleagues published a paper with the self-explanatory title When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End. The experiment behind it is simple. Participants put one hand in 14 degree C water for 60 seconds. The other hand went into the same cold water for 60 seconds, then stayed in for 30 more seconds as the water slowly warmed to 15 degrees, still unpleasant but gentler. Afterwards they were asked which trial they would repeat. Nearly 70% chose the longer one.

They chose 90 seconds of cold over 60 because memory does not add up like a ledger. Kahneman and colleagues called this the peak-end rule: how we remember an experience is dominated by its most intense moment and by how it ended. The middle, and the total length, mostly vanish. Some participants even reported that the longer trial had felt less cold overall or had taken less time.

The rule shows up outside the lab too. Kahneman and the Canadian physician Donald Redelmeier studied patients who had had a colonoscopy, a procedure that lasted anything from four to 69 minutes and is universally described as unpleasant. How long it went on had almost no bearing on how patients remembered it afterwards. What mattered was the discomfort at its worst moment, and the discomfort in the final minutes.

BBC Future's Tom Stafford draws a less obvious lesson. The interesting part is not that duration does not matter, but that we get to choose how to divide time into experiences. If a memorable weekend can feel as refreshing as a two-week holiday, the trick may be to carve life into distinct episodes with clean endings, rather than one long stretch that bleeds into the next in memory.
