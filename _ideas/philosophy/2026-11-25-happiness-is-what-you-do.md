Aristotle opens the Nicomachean Ethics with a question most of us skip: what is a good life actually? He assumes we can list the things that are good, friends, health, honour, a little wealth. The hard part is that these goods do not rank themselves, and we want to know which are most worth choosing. So he asks for the highest good, the thing we want for its own sake and for the sake of which everything else is worth having. He notes that everyone already agrees this is what the Greeks called eudaimonia, normally translated as 'happiness' or 'flourishing'. The key move is to treat it as an evaluative term, a way of saying 'living well', not as a description of a feeling.

To work out what this highest good is, he asks what the function of a human being is, the way we can say a knife's function is to cut, and a good knife does it well. We share with plants the capacity to grow, and with animals the capacity to perceive. What sets us apart is reason. So living well is the lifelong activity of the rational part of the soul in accordance with virtue. Aristotle insists this is activity, doing, and not a condition or state you possess.

That gives ethics a practical shape. You do not need a finished theory of the good to start living well. You do need practice and habit, and the ability to judge which course of action is best on each particular occasion. The good life is more like being a good musician, something with a standard that you improve at over a lifetime, than a mood to be chased or a reward that arrives when everything else has gone right.
