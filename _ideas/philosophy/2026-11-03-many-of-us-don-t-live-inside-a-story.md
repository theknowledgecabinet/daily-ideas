A very popular idea in psychology and philosophy holds that each person lives inside a story. We are, it is said, the stories we tell about ourselves. The neurologist Oliver Sacks wrote that we each construct and live a narrative, and that this narrative is us. The cognitive psychologist Jerome Bruner called the self a perpetually rewritten story. The social psychologist Dan McAdams and the philosopher Daniel Dennett made the same point in different versions. There is, Strawson observes, a remarkably robust consensus around it, and it is tied to the comforting assumption that self-storytelling is a good thing, necessary for a full life. The philosopher Galen Strawson, writing in Aeon, argues that this is simply false.

Here is the shape of his case. He draws a line between two kinds of people. Narrative people, in his capital-letter sense, naturally experience their life as having the form of a story, and live in and through that conception. Non-Narrative people are anti-Narrative by fundamental constitution. For them memory is fragmentary, the present dominates, and no inner voice stitches yesterday and tomorrow into a single plot. Life simply never takes a story-like shape. Strawson places himself firmly in this second camp.

His evidence is as literary as it is philosophical. Montaigne, perhaps the greatest autobiographer in the language, wrote that his memory was grotesquely faulty, and that he derived comfort from the infirmity. Virginia Woolf argued in Modern Fiction that a free writer would produce no plot, no catastrophe in the accepted style, because life is a luminous halo surrounding us rather than a series of gig lamps. Strawson grants that the sequence of events can matter for some people, in some cases. What he rejects is the sweeping claim that everyone is a novelist of the self.

The deeper concern is moral. The dramatist Henry Taylor observed in 1836 that an imaginative person sees in their life the story of their life, and ends up conducting themselves to make a good story rather than a good life. If narrative were a universal human need, that pressure to perform a coherent plot would sit on every one of us, a recipe for inauthenticity. Strawson's comfort is that it is not universal. Some of us are simply not in the story, and that is permitted.
