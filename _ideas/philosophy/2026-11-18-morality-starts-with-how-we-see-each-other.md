Most of us picture morality as a set of duties or rules: what we must do, and what we must not. The philosopher Iris Murdoch thought that got the priorities backwards. For her, the heart of the moral life is not what we choose to do, but how we see other people. We are always building up a picture of the world, and that picture shapes what we do next. See someone as your enemy, and you will start treating them as one.

The trouble is that our picture is rarely accurate. Murdoch, building on the French thinker Simone Weil, argued that the key moral act is attention, and that the main thing distorting it is the ego. The ego, in her words, is an anxious and self-obsessed thing. It builds what she called a veil of self-preoccupied fantasy, so that we see only what flatters or protects us. Her little example is a mother-in-law, M, who privately thinks of her daughter-in-law D as vulgar and childish. M behaves perfectly politely, yet Murdoch asks whether the snobbish picture itself is a moral failure. She thinks it is. Thinking of another person in a hostile, unfair way matters, even when it never reaches the outside world.

What undoes the distortion is, for Murdoch, attentive love. Love means something different from the giddy, selective feeling of the novels. It is a patient, generous effort to see another person as they actually are, for their own sake rather than in relation to our own needs. In her example, M sits with her own discomfort, notices the jealousy underneath the snobbery, and slowly comes to see D as refreshingly simple rather than vulgar. The effort of attention changes the picture. Murdoch was a novelist as well as a philosopher, and she is blunt that there are no quick fixes. We can only loosen the ego's hold a little at a time, through the repeated practice of looking outwards.
