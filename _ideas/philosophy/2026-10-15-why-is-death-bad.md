We mostly take it for granted that dying is bad, at least most of the time. But the essay presses on a question we rarely stop to ask: what exactly does bad mean here, and does the question even make sense?

The strongest challenge comes from the Greek philosopher Epicurus, who argued that death is 'nothing to us.' While we are alive, death is not yet present, and when it does arrive we no longer exist to experience it. There is no moment in which being dead does us any harm. On this reading, our fear of our own death resembles being afraid of the number thirteen: we are safe, but the idea unsettles us for no good reason.

The trouble is that this conclusion is very hard to live with. If death can never harm us, it can never help us either, and yet that thought feels wrong. The essay's answer is that death does its harm through loss rather than through experience. Being dead involves no experience at all. What matters is what it closes off: the future of good things we would otherwise have had, the people we would have spent time with, the things we would have done.

This reframing carries a quiet consequence. Since there is no single fixed future the dead would otherwise have had, we can never say exactly how bad a given death is. We face many possible futures, good and bad and merely ordinary. We know that dying is usually bad and we understand what makes it so. But the precise size of the loss stays beyond our grasp.
