Marcus Aurelius wrote the Meditations not for publication but as a private notebook for himself, and it keeps returning to one trick. When a worry takes hold, zoom out. You stand in your street, then see the street from above, then your town, then your country, then the whole planet, then the Earth as a small point among the stars. The trouble you were carrying stays the same size, but your frame does not. In one passage (Book 9, section 32), he notes that most of what disturbs him lies entirely in his own judgment, and that he can free himself up by taking in the whole universe with his mind and contemplating the eternity of time, how brief the span is from birth to dissolution, and how boundless the time was before birth and will be after it.

The exercise works on two axes at once, space and time. The spatial version, the one the Stoics called the view from above, was a standard Hellenistic practice, not a Marcus invention. Pierre Hadot, the scholar who did more than anyone to document ancient spiritual exercises, describes the aim as relocating human existence within the infinity of time and space, so that the philosopher is not disturbed by the small things that cause so much suffering. The temporal version, which the passages above lean on, adds the point that you existed nowhere before you were born and will exist nowhere after you die. Your career, the argument, the inbox, all of it sits inside a slice of time that is barely visible from that distance.

Fideler's essay makes one extra point that I like. When the space program finally put people up high enough to do this for real, the effect matched the ancient description. Seeing the Earth from space, with no borders visible and the whole of life packed into one fragile disc, is the view from above executed on hardware. The Stoics ran it on imagination, but the destination is the same.
