Inside the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider at Brookhaven, two metal rings more than two miles around send clouds of gold ions hurtling in opposite directions until they collide. The fireball that results sits around four trillion degrees Celsius, more than 250,000 times hotter than the core of the sun, and for a sliver of time far shorter than a blink, the protons and neutrons that make up ordinary matter melt into their components, quarks and gluons. Physicists call that soup the quark-gluon plasma, and before 2000 it had only ever existed in the first few microseconds after the Big Bang.

The surprise came when the results refused to behave. Standard physics says that heat a substance enough and it becomes a gas, and early theory predicted the plasma would do the same, a hot gas of quarks moving freely and ignoring each other. The data from RHIC showed the opposite. Particles scattered in elliptical flow patterns, a signature of strong interaction, and jets of particles lost far more energy travelling through the plasma than models expected. In 2005 the four detector teams announced they had found "the most perfect liquid ever observed", a state with almost zero resistance to flow, its particles moving in coordination like mercury, only more so. For many of the scientists, it was a revelation.

CERN's Large Hadron Collider later made the same plasma at about 50 percent hotter and confirmed the same liquid behaviour, which moved the result beyond any single lab's quirk. RHIC has now completed its final run, and the lab is converting it into the Electron-Ion Collider, which will study the cold, dense matter we live in instead of the early universe. Abhay Deshpande, now at Brookhaven, has a line about the kind of day experimentalists dream of: the day when a lab result is completely different from what theory says, because that is when you learn something new.
