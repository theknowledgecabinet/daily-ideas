In the 1988 classic A Brief History of Time, Stephen Hawking spends a chapter on the arrow of time, and it makes for a better argument than most books about time management.

The starting point is a puzzle. The basic laws of physics do not pick a direction: film two gas molecules bouncing off each other and play the film backwards, and nothing in the laws tells you which version actually happened. So why does time feel like it flows? Hawking points out that a broken cup never reassembles because entropy, a measure of disorder, only tends to increase. A cup shattering is a routine step in that direction. The reverse would be a violation the second law treats as overwhelmingly unlikely.

He then ties the feeling of time to the physics. The direction we remember, he argues, is not a separate mystery from the direction entropy goes: we remember the past because that is the direction in which entropy has already risen, and we never remember the future for the same reason. He also notes that the universe itself points one way, since it is expanding rather than contracting. All of these arrows, in his account, line up, and the alignment is what makes cause and effect possible.

A stranger idea in the same book supports the point. Hawking and Roger Penrose argued the universe began in a singularity, and Hawking later developed the no boundary proposal, in which the universe is finite but has no edge in time. In that picture there is no moment of creation to point backwards from, and asking what came before the Big Bang is like asking what lies north of the North Pole. The one way of time, then, is not built into the laws. It comes from where the universe happened to start: smooth, ordered, and heading towards disorder.
