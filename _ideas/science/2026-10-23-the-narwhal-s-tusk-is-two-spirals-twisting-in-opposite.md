The narwhal's tusk is the left canine tooth, grown through the jaw and lip, and it can reach more than two metres while staying straight and left-handed. It has no enamel. The inside is dentine, the outside is cementum. For centuries people admired the shape, and medieval traders sold the tusks as unicorn horns, but no one could map what sits inside.

A team led by Aarhus University's Henrik Birkedal and Adrian Rodriguez-Palomo did, using 3D X-ray techniques called tensor tomography, and they had to borrow beamtime from three big synchrotrons: MAX IV in Sweden, the Swiss Light Source, and the ESRF in France. The tusks came from Greenland's collections.

The answer is that the tooth contains two spirals, not one. The mineralised collagen fibrils in the outer cementum twist into a left-handed helix. The fibrils in the inner dentine twist into a right-handed helix. The two meet at the boundary between dentine and cementum, which turns out to be a more intricate biological interface than anyone had assumed. The counterbalancing arrangement makes the tusk far more stable against bending and twisting than a single spiral or a straight rod would be. An architecture like this is found in other biological materials that have to take large forces.

What makes the pattern striking is how constant it is. The double spiral repeats across the tusk's annual growth layers, a bit like tree rings but with the same twist every year. The team says this suggests the left-handed growth is genetically programmed and stable across the animal's life, which can run to about 80 years. Birkedal's group is now checking whether the tusk records environmental changes over a whale's life, which would make each tusk a kind of climate archive.

The function of the tusk remains open. Most researchers think it is a sexual signal, since it is usually males that grow one, though a few females do. Proposed sensory or hunting roles have so far not found good evidence in the animals' behaviour.
