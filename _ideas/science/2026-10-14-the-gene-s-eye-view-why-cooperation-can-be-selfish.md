In 1976 Richard Dawkins repackaged Darwin's theory around a single move: look at evolution from the point of view of the gene. Genes, he argued, are the fundamental unit of selection. They are "selfish" not in any moral sense but because natural selection operates on genes competing to make copies of themselves. The organisms we see, lions and humans and dandelions, are better thought of as vehicles, disposable robots built to carry their genes to the next generation.

It is a reframe, and reframes do real work. Seen from the body, evolution looks like individuals and groups fighting for their own good. Seen from the gene, what looks like self-sacrifice stops being mysterious. A gene that makes you die helping your sister can still spread, because your sister is very likely to carry a copy of that very gene and pass it on. This is the logic of kin selection, the idea W. D. Hamilton had been developing in the 1960s, and the reason bees and wasps share a colony and die for it.

Dawkins is careful. "Selfish" names a mechanism of inheritance, not a moral claim. Genes do not want, plan or prefer anything. And the view has limits: modern genetics treats whole populations and organisms as units of selection too, not single genes alone. Still, the reframe has survived four decades and is now standard. It is also why the same book introduced the meme, its cultural cousin, as a unit of imitation in 1976.
