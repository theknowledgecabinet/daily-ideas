On 6 October 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz of the University of Geneva announced the first detection of a planet orbiting a star like the Sun. They had been measuring tiny wobbles in the star 51 Pegasi with ELODIE, a spectrograph on the 1.93-metre telescope at the Observatoire de Haute-Provence in southern France. As the star wobbles, its light shifts slightly towards and then away from Earth, because an unseen body is tugging on it. It is the seesaw logic: the heavier partner sits near the pivot.

What made the 1995 result strange was the kind of planet it was. 51 Pegasi b is a gas giant of roughly half Jupiter's mass, completing an orbit in a little over four days, with a sky that burns at about 1,000 degrees Celsius. The formation models of the time held that gas giants could only build themselves in the cold outer regions of a system, where icy material is available. The discovery contradicted that picture, and the view that has since prevailed is that such planets form further out and then migrate inward to their close orbits. A single odd planet proved nothing by itself, but it forced the theory of how planetary systems form to be rewritten.

Scepticism was real at the time. The wobble signal could have come from a pulsating star instead of a planet, and the astronomer David F. Gray initially argued for that reading before retracting it in 1998. Confirmation came when other teams found similar signals in their own data. Paul Butler and Geoffrey Marcy at Berkeley announced the second such planet, 70 Virginis b, in 1996. Since then the confirmed count of planets around other stars has passed 6,000, and the statistics suggest that almost every star in the Milky Way has at least one.
