takeaways:
- Most scientific work is puzzle-solving inside an agreed framework that the community rarely questions, rather than the slow accumulation of facts.
- A new framework usually wins over several generations, as independent breakthroughs make each other more credible, instead of through one decisive experiment.
- Because a new framework changes what counts as a valid question, the old and the new often cannot be compared directly, which is why debates inside a field can feel like people talking past each other.
caveats: Kuhn is describing how science has often worked, not laying down a strict rule, and the word paradigm has drifted far from his technical sense. His stronger claim of incommensurability has been contested by philosophers of science.
try_this: Next time someone says they are living through a paradigm shift, ask whether there is a working alternative framework with its own results, or just a new vocabulary. It takes a minute and is a fair test.

In 1947, a young Thomas Kuhn at Harvard was asked to teach a science class for humanities students using historical case studies. He picked up Aristotle's Physics and, reading it as a Newtonian, found it full of errors of logic and observation. Then it struck him: Aristotle was not a bad Newton. He was a brilliant scientist reasoning inside a different set of assumptions about matter and motion. That insight became the foundation of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, published in 1962.

Kuhn's core claim is that science does not progress by steadily accumulating facts. Most of the time it does what he called normal science, which is puzzle-solving inside an agreed framework, a paradigm. Scientists rarely question the framework. When observations do not fit, they assume the framework is right and the experiment is wrong. Only when such anomalies pile up into a crisis do some bolder members of the community start working on alternatives, while most stay conservative. Kuhn thought that was a good thing.

His famous example, the Copernican Revolution, undercuts the standard story. Copernicus's sun-centred model initially predicted planetary positions no better than the old Ptolemaic system, and Kuhn judged that his contemporaries were right to reject it. The shift happened over generations: Galileo conjectured that objects do not stop unless friction slows them, Kepler found that planets move in ellipses, and Newton later unified the two. The old and new frameworks also change the questions that count as worth asking, and the terms used, so they cannot be compared neutrally. Kuhn called them incommensurable: "The competition between paradigms is not the sort of battle that can be resolved by proofs."
